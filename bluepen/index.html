Motivation

Personalization often starts from known user information, such as demographics, interaction history, or observed behavior1. For a new user, however, the information that matters may still be missing. Preferences may emerge through interaction history over time, but we can also ask the user directly. Learning to conduct these conversations presents two challenges.

1Salemi et al., 2024LaMP: When Large Language Models Meet Personalization

Challenge 1: Learning which questions are useful

The quality of a final recommendation provides feedback on the conversation as a whole, without identifying which questions uncovered important preferences. Prior work selects questions using explicit models of user preferences2,3 or estimates their contribution through simulated future conversations4. These approaches require a preference model or repeated simulations, which can be computationally expensive.

2Bose et al., 2026Cold-Start Personalization via Bayesian Adaptive Questioning
3Choudhury et al., 2026BED-LLM: Bayesian Experimental Design
4Wu et al., 2025CollabLLM: From Passive Responders to Active Collaborators

Challenge 2: Learning when to stop

A fixed interaction budget cannot adapt to how much information different users reveal5. One user may share several relevant preferences immediately, while another needs targeted follow-ups. Value-of-information approaches instead estimate whether another question’s expected benefit outweighs its cost to the user6. However, predicting the usefulness of an unseen answer and the effort required from the user is difficult. The model must learn to stop before further questioning adds effort without improving personalization.

5Li et al., 2026PrefDisco: Proactive Personalized Reasoning
6Dong et al., 2026Value of Information: Human–Agent Communication

BluePen

Training

BluePen* learns both what to ask and when to stop. It samples conversations with each simulated user, then uses the completed conversations and privileged user information to critique individual questions.

*“Blue Pen” or Blue Pencil-ing refers to the traditional copy-editing practice where editors marked private feedback on journalist and interviewers’ copy in a light blue that did not register on early photocopiers or photographic film scans.

The same model uses these critiques for self-distillation7, providing token-level guidance on what to ask without exposing the hidden user profile to the interviewer.

7Hübotter et al., 2026Reinforcement Learning via Self-Distillation

We combine this guidance with GRPO8, using downstream task rewards and conversation-length penalties. GRPO trains both question and stopping tokens, so the model learns to end the conversation when it has enough information for personalization.

Results

We evaluate BluePen on three tasks: exercise prediction9, medical question answering10, and book recommendation11. Each task requires the model to elicit different information from users. We evaluate on held-out users, with simulator behavior controls matched across methods.

9Wan et al., 2025Personalized Dialogue with Curiosity Reward
10Li et al., 2024MediQ: Interactive Clinical Reasoning
11Hou et al., 2026Amazon Reviews 2023 / BLaIR

Main results

BluePen achieves the highest downstream scores among conversation-based methods across all three tasks. It improves over the strongest baseline by 4.21 points on ExercisePred, 7.42 points on MediQ, and 1.64 points on BookRec. These comparisons are against GRPO for ExercisePred and MediQ, and DPO for BookRec. BluePen uses fewer turns than these baselines on MediQ and BookRec, and slightly more on ExercisePred.

Stopping criteria results

To examine when the model stops, we score shorter conversation prefixes and then force the model to continue beyond its chosen stopping point, Q. Ideally, once the model stops, additional questions should not improve downstream personalization. Stopping two turns earlier reduces BluePen’s performance across all three tasks. Adding two more turns produces little further benefit: approximately +1.0%, +1.0%, and −0.3% relative to its stopping-point score on ExercisePred, MediQ, and BookRec, respectively.

How the curves are measured

These are the paper’s trajectory-weighted curves, using complete, successfully scored continuations at all five positions. Each curve shows 100 × (mean score at the selected position − mean score at Q) / mean score at Q. Methods have different retained user cohorts; the results describe this continuation experiment, rather than proving an optimal stopping point for every user.
