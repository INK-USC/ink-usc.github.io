Arbor: Locating Reasoning Failures in Long Chains of Thought
through Reasoning State Tracking

Every chain of thought becomes a tree of reasoning states, and every failure is a relation between a step and the state before it.

1University of Southern California, 2The Chinese University of Hong Kong, 3New York University

Abstract

Large reasoning models solve complex problems through long chains of thought, in which prior work has documented failure modes such as repetition, underthinking, and unsupported deductions. Existing methods detect these failures from local signals: textual similarity to nearby steps, switch tokens, or an LLM's judgment of a step's correctness. However, what a step contributes to the solution depends on the state that earlier steps have established, so identifying failures requires a global view that tracks that state across the trajectory.

Therefore, we propose Arbor, a reasoning-failure attribution framework that represents each trajectory as a tree of reasoning states, built as each step creates, verifies, prunes, or revisits a state, and attributes each failure to a relation between a step and the state preceding it. Using Arbor, we refine the three failure modes into distinct subtypes that harm reasoning through different mechanisms and at different costs. We find that the tree state Arbor maintains before a step separates failure steps from the at-risk steps at which the failure did not occur. We discuss how Arbor integrates with existing mitigations at inference time and in training data.

A trajectory as a tree of reasoning states. We define reasoning failures relative to the state of the reasoning, the intermediate results established, the branches open and the constraints in force when a step occurs. We represent each trajectory as a tree of reasoning states, in which nodes are intermediate results, each edge records the node a step derives from, and where a step attaches identifies a failure.

How Arbor works

Three stages. Arbor (Attributing Reasoning failures By Operations on Reasoning-state trees) turns a trajectory into a tree of reasoning states in three stages: a parser maps each step to an operation on a schema-grounded payload; a deterministic engine resolves the step to a node of the tree built so far and applies one mutation; and a failure row is a predicate on the step, the node it resolves to and the tree before it. Only the parser calls a model; the same parse always replays to the same tree and the same labels, and every label names the earlier node it is defined against.

Eleven failure rows. Prior work has documented three recurring failure modes in long chains of thought: repetition, in which the model re-reasons about results it has already established; underthinking, in which it abandons a line of reasoning that could still succeed; and unsupported deduction, steps the preceding context does not entail. Each mode, however, covers steps that arise through different mechanisms and harm reasoning in different ways. Arbor splits the three modes into eleven rows; the table below lists them, each with a trajectory in which you can watch it happen.

Three tasks. We instantiate the framework on three reasoning tasks with distinct reasoning structures: ZebraLogic, constraint satisfaction; the 24 Game, search over arithmetic compositions; and PlanBench, sequential action planning under preconditions.

See each failure on a tree

Every row below opens one trajectory at the thought that carries the failure. The page lands on that thought and the node it created, and each click reveals one sentence of the story and points to the next click: where the state came from, what happened in between, and what followed.

A tree of reasoning states from the demo. Nodes are intermediate results, labelled in plain words. Click to open the R1 Re-Derivation page, or pick a row below.

A guided walkthrough of each failure. Each page is an interactive, guided tour: it shows how every thought maps to a change on the tree, the state each node holds, and the relation that makes a step a failure. Follow the prompts click by click and the failure unfolds one step at a time.
